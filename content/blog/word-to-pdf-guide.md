# Convert Word to PDF: Preserve Formatting Every Time

You've spent hours perfecting your Word document. The margins are exact, the fonts are consistent, the images are aligned. Then you send it to someone, and they open it in a different version of Word — or worse, in another word processor — and everything is scrambled. Converting to PDF solves this problem permanently. In this guide, we'll cover how to convert Word documents to PDF while preserving every detail of your formatting.

## Why Convert Word to PDF?

The PDF format was designed for exactly this purpose: a document that looks the same everywhere. When you convert Word to PDF:

- **Fonts are embedded** so the document looks correct even if the recipient doesn't have your fonts installed.
- **Layout is locked** — margins, page breaks, image positions, and text flow are preserved exactly as you designed them.
- **Universal readability** across Windows, Mac, Linux, iOS, Android, and any modern web browser.
- **Security** — PDFs can be password-protected and are harder to edit than Word documents.
- **Professional presentation** — PDFs look polished and final. A Word document can look like a draft; a PDF looks like a deliverable.

## Common Conversion Problems (and How to Avoid Them)

Bad conversions happen. Here are the most common issues and how to prevent them:

### Missing Fonts
If the conversion tool doesn't embed your fonts, the PDF will substitute whatever fonts are available on the viewing device. Your carefully-chosen Merriweather becomes generic Times New Roman.

**Fix:** Use a conversion tool that embeds fonts, like SaveVex. Avoid exotic fonts that may not embed correctly — test with one page first if you're unsure.

### Shifted Page Breaks
Word's pagination depends on the active printer driver and page setup. A different conversion engine may calculate page breaks differently.

**Fix:** Use manual page breaks (Ctrl+Enter) rather than relying on automatic pagination for critical breaks. Check the PDF's page count matches the original.

### Broken Image Placement
Images anchored to specific positions in Word may shift during conversion, landing on the wrong page or overlapping text.

**Fix:** Use "In Line with Text" wrapping for images whenever possible. This positioning is the most reliably preserved during conversion. Avoid floating images with tight text wrapping for documents destined for PDF.

### Lost Hyperlinks
Internal links (table of contents, cross-references) and external URLs need to be preserved as clickable links in the PDF.

**Fix:** Verify that your conversion tool maintains hyperlinks. SaveVex preserves both internal and external links, but it's always worth spot-checking a few after conversion.

## Pre-Conversion Checklist

Before you convert, run through these checks to ensure a smooth result:

- [ ] **Spell-check complete.** Fix typos now — editing a PDF is harder.
- [ ] **All images are embedded, not linked.** Linked images will be missing from the PDF.
- [ ] **Manual page breaks are in place** where exact pagination matters.
- [ ] **Table of contents is updated** — refresh the TOC field before converting so page numbers are current.
- [ ] **Headers and footers are consistent** across sections.
- [ ] **Fonts are standard or tested.** Fancy script fonts sometimes embed incorrectly.
- [ ] **Document properties are set** — title, author, and subject metadata carry over to the PDF.

## What About DOC vs DOCX?

Word supports two formats: the legacy `.doc` (Word 97-2003) and the modern `.docx` (Word 2007+). Here's what you need to know:

- **DOCX is preferred.** It's an open XML-based format that converts more reliably to PDF. All modern versions of Word use DOCX by default.
- **DOC still works** but may have more formatting quirks during conversion. If possible, open the DOC file in a modern word processor, save as DOCX, then convert to PDF.
- **If you only have DOC files**, SaveVex can convert them directly. Just review the output more carefully for any formatting shifts.
